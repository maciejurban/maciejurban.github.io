## The bet

The bet was consolidation, not features. [[ORG]] had several separate things —
360s, reviews, signatures, renaming — and customers were bending them to get
what they actually wanted: signatures on a 360, a renamed 360, transitions
between one type and another. The decision was that one configurable system is
easier to maintain than five rigid ones.

The stake was every existing customer. This replaced a system that people ran
legally and financially consequential processes on. In France a performance
review is a legally required document, so the thing being replaced was not a
screen. It was an obligation someone had already met.

A year to beta. Eighteen months to leave it. Four to five people. That is not
delay; it is what replacing a load-bearing system costs when you may not break a
single in-flight process.

<Beat n={2} title="The base model">

A campaign runs in stages: responses, then a review meeting made of
preparations, the meeting itself and a summary, then signatures. The people are
a reviewee, a manager, peers, a note-taker and a review supervisor. Visibility
is controlled per question, so for each question you decide what each reviewer
can see. Campaign dates drive step dates, which means the state of a review
depends on the calendar as much as on what anyone has done.

### The primitive

My contribution to this was a reduction. A step is a form, a set of users, and
permissions. Before, a step was a stage you pass through. After, it is an object
with structure, and objects with structure compose. The team then built more on
that abstraction than I designed: reminders that hang off steps, further
mechanisms on the same shape.

The reduction is what kills the wizard. A wizard has to ask about everything at
campaign level — define the reminders for the campaign, define who sees what for
the campaign — because it has nowhere else to put the question. When a step is
self-contained, each question gets asked where it makes sense. Pick the form.
Pick who. Pick what they see. Next step. Configuration stops being a
questionnaire about the system and becomes a description of the process.

The test an abstraction has to survive is the degenerate case. Signatures are
just a checkbox. They still have a form, still have users, still have
permissions, still the same object. Most abstractions fail that test.

</Beat>

<Beat n={3} title="The scene that reframed the project">

There was no incident and no escalation. The team's method was to write out
every case they could imagine and then go and research whether those cases
actually occur. This one they found on Slack. Someone changed a skill and
mentioned it in passing. Everyone saw the message, went to check their own
reviews, and found descriptions that were not the ones they had answered
against.

Nothing was broken. The system did exactly what it had been built to do: show
the current definition of the skill. What surfaced was a missing theory of time.

The reframe came from that. A review is not a screen to fill in. It is a
document about how someone did their work over three months, and it may matter
later — to the employee, to the employer, in a dispute, and in France as a legal
requirement. A document like that is amended, not overwritten. "Just update it
to the latest data" stops being a convenience and becomes falsification.

The inversion is worth sitting with, because it runs against the instinct of
nearly every system anyone has built: normally fresher data is better data. Here
fresher data destroys the value of the record. And the rule that follows is
about people, not merge strategies. A discrepancy is a problem for a person to
resolve, not a change that should enter the review by itself.

</Beat>

<Beat n={4} title="The reconciliation model">

The model that came out of it has four moves. Snapshot the answers against the
definitions they were written for. Detect the diff when a definition moves.
Route that diff to a permission that can understand it, because the person who
can judge whether a changed skill description still describes the same work is
not always the person looking at the screen. Then resolve, and resolution has
three outcomes rather than one: some diffs apply safely, some invalidate
preparations that have already been written, and some cannot be applied at all,
because the skill or the level they referred to no longer exists.

That third outcome is the one that forces the design to be honest. There is no
correct automatic answer to a question whose subject has been deleted, so the
system has to be able to hold a review in a state that says so.

<Artifact caption="The reconciliation model: snapshot, diff detection, routing by permission, and the three resolution outcomes — applies cleanly, invalidates preparations, cannot be applied." ratio="4/3" />

</Beat>

<Beat n={5} title="The combinatorial chain">

Beta customers asked for something small and reasonable. Steps should close
themselves once they are filled, instead of waiting for an admin to close them.
Manual closing looked like friction.

It was not friction. It was a checkpoint. The admin closing the step was also,
incidentally, the human verifying that the state was sane.

Walk the consequence and it is short, which is the argument. The step closes
itself, so skills apply with no human in the loop. A source change can now land
while nobody is looking. So the system has to detect that a form has gone stale.
So it has to tell the note-taker. The note-taker can see the problem and has no
permission to fix it. So the copy has to route to the admin. So you need a
distinct stale state, an escalation path, and copy that does not dead-end.

One beta request. Five new exceptions, each one a consequence of the last.

<Pullquote>

When you remove a manual step, you remove the person who was also keeping the
state honest, and you have to rebuild that supervision in the system or it is
simply gone.

</Pullquote>

<Artifact caption="The stale-state escalation: what the note-taker sees, what they can and cannot act on, and the route to the admin who can." ratio="16/9" />

This is where the project's effort actually went. The team's own estimate is
that exceptions accounted for roughly two thirds of the work — an estimate, not
a measurement, and worth treating as one. The happy path was never the expensive
part.

### The boundary not crossed

Almost every exception was handled. One was not. You cannot reopen a closed
review and annex a change to it. In production, closed is closed.

<Aside>

This is neither a clean decision nor pure debt. It is a frozen debate. There are
concepts on the table — skill version history producing snapshots, editing past
entries — and each one opens a further class of problems: how to handle editing
an edit, who is permitted to annex, what happens to signatures already on the
document. A team of four could not take that on while trying to leave beta.

</Aside>

The motive for wanting it is not customer convenience. The model should reflect
the truth, and a person should be able to understand why their skill looks
different today than it did in a review six months ago. An unexplained gap in
the record is itself the problem.

The concept is more interesting than a workaround, so it is worth naming
properly. Do not edit the closed review. Read its skill snapshot, create a new
review on the old versions, and append a history entry dated today as an annex.
The original stays untouched, the correction is a separate document, and
subsequent changes accept onto the annex. Accounting does this with reversing
entries. Law does it with contract amendments.

</Beat>

<Beat n={6} title="What shipped and what it did">

Of the organizations given the beta, 70 to 80% adopted it. The only blocker is
missing feature parity. Not the model, not comprehension, not resistance. That
distinction is worth holding onto, because it makes the remaining work a
scheduling problem rather than a design one, and those fail in different ways.

Those two facts together say something fairly precise. The model works, and the
cost of the model was that building a new engine obliged the team to rebuild
everything that had grown around the old one: reminders, bulk actions, reports,
migrations, combined views. Things the previous system simply had.

Not all of it was recreation. New capability shipped alongside — better data
visualization, filtering that did not exist before, process-flow construction,
higher-level access management for reviews, administrators and viewers, control
of the review flow between steps, step renaming.

The volume of work exceeded what the team can deliver, and the project
continues.

</Beat>

<Beat n={7} title="What I'd do differently" final={true}>

Given the resources the team actually has, I would not have replaced the engine.
Take the scattered types that already exist — legacy campaign, 360, 1:1, review
with preparations — leave them where they are, and wrap them in a layer that
solves what was genuinely missing: sending, and combining the results.
Consolidate at the orchestration and data layer instead of underneath.

There is a rhyme for this in the surveys product, where the journey type
collapsed into a one-off survey sent by a separate feature. Integration by
sending turned out to be enough, and the separate type proved unnecessary.

The decision to build a new engine was an architectural one, taken outside
design. Its cost surfaced only at the scale of a year, which is the scale at
which "we will rebuild the surrounding features as we go" stops being a plan and
becomes the project.

</Beat>
