<Beat n={2} title="Surveys are workflows">

A survey looks like a form with a send button, and almost nothing about running
one at company scale behaves that way. There is the definition and there are its
runs, which are different objects with different lifetimes. There is who may
create and who may see results, which are different permissions and are
constantly mistaken for one another. There are anonymity thresholds, below which
results must not be shown at all. There is recurrence, which turns a single
artefact into a series that has to stay comparable with itself.

Each of those is a state machine. None of them is visible if the mental model is
a form.

The anonymity threshold is the clearest example. Below it, results exist and
must not be shown, which is a different state from having no results yet and a
different state again from the run not having closed. Three situations, one
empty screen, unless the product is built to tell them apart. Get that wrong and
an administrator concludes that nobody answered, which is both false and exactly
the kind of false that erodes confidence in the instrument.

The setup this replaced was a multi-step wizard, and the general point is worth
stating without reference to how it came to exist: a wizard optimizes the first
pass. Administrative software has to optimize the return — coming back,
inspecting something, changing one subsection, leaving.

</Beat>

<Beat n={3} title="The measurement problem">

The same question shows up in three contexts, at volumes that are not remotely
alike. The shape of a typical week at this scale, offered as illustration rather
than as anything observed: whole-company recurring listening produces on the
order of a hundred-plus answers; onboarding produces a handful, from people
still in honeymoon mode; a one-off campaign produces a few dozen, from people
who selected themselves into it by caring about the topic.

Average those together and the chart comes out clean, and lies. The clean part
is the problem. None of the three inputs is the thing the blended number claims
to be: one is close to a population sample, one is a biased sample of a biased
moment, and one is self-selected. The average has no referent in the world. It
is a number about the arithmetic, not about the company.

Small organizations pay for this twice. They make decisions on noise, and then
they lose trust in the instrument the first time a number moves without anything
having changed in the actual workplace. The second cost is the expensive one,
because it is not recoverable by fixing the chart.

</Beat>

<Beat n={4} title="The architecture">

Questions are the unit of measurement. Surveys are delivery contexts, not
containers. Same question text means the same measurement anywhere it appears,
which is what makes any comparison across contexts legitimate in the first
place.

The rules that hold that together are small and unglamorous. Once a question has
answers, its text is locked; editing produces a new version rather than
rewriting history under the answers already given. Without that rule, a
seemingly harmless wording change turns every historical answer into a response
to a question nobody was asked, and the trend line that runs through it becomes
a chart of two different things drawn as one. All scale questions run in one
direction, five is positive, and there are no inverted items — reverse-coded
questions are a research convention that buys a little response-bias control and
costs every downstream consumer a correctness trap. Tags organize the library,
because a question genuinely belongs to several topics at once and a tree would
force a false choice about which one is primary. Dimensions exist only in the
recurring pulse context, where a stable question set measured repeatedly over
time makes the extra structure worth its cost, and nowhere else. Applying
dimensions everywhere was the obvious generalization and would have meant
maintaining a taxonomy for contexts that never produce enough data to populate
it.

Then there is what production settled on, which is stronger than the rule the
design started with. Sources are never merged into one number, and they are
fully comparable: a score per source, results plotted per source, side by side.
Compare freely, never merge. Attribution is never lost, so a number on a chart
can always answer the question of who was asked, when, and in what context.

<Beat n={5} title="What was cut, and where the model stops">

Cut on purpose: dimensions in every survey type, event-triggered journeys,
automatically blended cross-source trends, question-rotation machinery. The
blended trends are the interesting omission, because they are the feature a
buyer would ask for and the one the architecture exists to refuse.

Question rotation is the one I would expect to come back. Rotating a subset of
questions each cycle is how you keep a recurring survey short without shrinking
the question set, and the price is that each cycle measures a slightly different
thing. That trade is worth making at a scale where fatigue is the binding
constraint, and it is not worth making before then, when comparability is what
the product is trying to establish in the first place.

Where the model stops working is easier to state than most people state it.
Above roughly a thousand employees, asking everyone everything stops being
viable and you need matrix sampling. Multi-country needs translations and, with
them, per-locale measurement integrity, which is a harder problem than
translation. Enterprise needs benchmarks, role-based access, and a per-answer
audit trail.

One thing shrank in contact with reality. The design called for three survey
types. In production the journey type collapsed into a simple one-off survey
sent by a separate feature, and integration by sending turned out to be
sufficient. One primitive fewer than designed, which is the direction a design
should be wrong in.

</Beat>

<Beat n={6} title="The builder">

The argument that settles the interface is not about efficiency. HR has to
validate the whole process before sending. The survey goes to the entire
company, it cannot be recalled, and what is on the line is everyone's confidence
that the person who sent it knew what they were doing. Someone who has to be
certain needs to see the whole thing at once. A wizard shows one step and hides
the rest, and so does splitting build, cycles and send into separate paths. The
interface follows from where the irreversible moment sits in this product.

So the builder is itself the summary. Each card carries the state of one part of
the survey — configured, incomplete, or not applicable to this type — and is
also the entry point to editing it, with sub-views opening for detail. Nothing
sits behind a sequence, so the check before sending is a single read of one
screen rather than a walk back through a sequence.

<Artifact caption="The card builder: each card shows the state of one part of the survey and opens a sub-view for detail, so the whole configuration is legible at once." ratio="16/9" />

The honest limits matter here. Testing showed that navigation is not a problem.
That is a narrow finding and it should be read narrowly. It says nothing about
whether people understand what they configured, or whether they could
reconstruct their own intent a month later, which is the thing that actually
determines whether a recurring survey stays coherent. The open risk is that
people habituated to other builders find this confusing precisely because it
does not march them through.

The cost of the path is not in doubt and neither is the choice. There is no
prior art to copy here, hard moments are likely, and the beaten track would have
been easier. That is what the choice forgoes, and it was taken anyway.

Worth noting once: this card pattern now appears in two products, a year apart.
That makes it a pattern rather than a one-off.

### The AI layer, parked

The AI layer is a concept and nothing more. It is unimplemented and held on EU
regulatory grounds, and the permission model — what an assistant may change and
what it may not — is exactly the open question rather than an implementation
detail to settle later. AI systems used in employment decisions fall under
high-risk classification, and this is precisely that domain.

### The library, and what the recurring pulse is for

The question library is in build and currently stalled on staffing: the team is
on the builder and on maintenance first. In the meantime [[PRODUCT:pulse]], the
recurring survey, is treated as a place to collect data between cycles, and the
cycles themselves as proto-sources.

The order is inverted from the obvious one. Rather than design the library and
then go looking at the data, collect within a controlled range first, so that
what the library has to deliver is derived from views someone actually needed.

</Beat>

<Beat n={7} title="Where this stands" final={true}>

The substrate argument was won. Questions are the unit, sources stay attributed,
and the rules that make comparison legitimate are in the product rather than in
a document about the product. The substrate is now waiting on people: the
library is stalled on staffing, and the assistant that motivated the whole thing
is parked on regulatory grounds.

That is the honest end state, and it is not a regret. The thing worth watching
is whether an architecture whose payoff is deferred can survive a long enough
wait — because the value of a substrate is not visible until something is built
on top of it, and nothing has been yet.
