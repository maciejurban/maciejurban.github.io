I was hired for this as a 0→1 project. I invented [[PRODUCT:careershub]] and I
designed it.

<Beat n={2} title="The problem">

The old builder let customers do anything, and a great many of them had. The
volume of custom code written against it was enormous, so every platform change
risked breaking a live career site. That produced an endless support load and a
recurring argument, because customers who had built custom did not accept that
building custom meant owning the breakage.

The mechanism underneath is worth naming, because it is not specific to careers
sites. Once customers write code against your markup, your markup becomes an
interface. You never designed it as one, you never versioned it, and you cannot
test against it, because the code that depends on it lives somewhere you cannot
see. Every release turns into a compatibility question with no way to answer it
in advance.

That is also why the argument with customers never resolved. They experienced it
as the platform breaking their site. The team experienced it as customer code
depending on something nobody had promised. Both descriptions are accurate, and
neither one gives anybody a next move.

This is what unbounded flexibility actually costs. It does not arrive as one bad
decision. It arrives as a permanent tax on every subsequent release.

</Beat>

<Beat n={3} title="Constrained composition, not a canvas">

There was no budget for real section editing. That constraint decided the shape
of the answer, and it produced a better product than the budget would have. The
obvious way to spend money on a page builder is to make the canvas better. The
constraint forced a different question: what is the smallest set of moves that
covers what customers actually make?

Each section offers two or three ready layouts. Inside a section there is a
swappable primary element — heading at the top, on the left, on the right, or
absent — and because it is WYSIWYG, swapping it also replaces the text. The
customer is therefore never handed an empty field to fill; every choice is
between things that already look finished.

Below that sits a swappable main block, itself configurable: galleries with
presets, job sections rendered as a table or as cards, cards with or without a
description or a button, a configurable application form, control over which
details appear where.

Elements carry smart actions rather than raw controls. Enlarge this image.
Change the grid. Let this image take a two-by-two and reflow the rest around it.
The difference matters more than it looks. A raw control asks the customer to
hold the layout in their head and express an intention as a number. A smart
action states the intention and leaves the layout to the system, which is the
only reason a bounded builder can feel quicker than a free one instead of merely
safer. Safety on its own loses to freedom every time. Speed plus safety does
not.

Defaults reinforce the same thing by always doing what you would have chosen
anyway. Over the top of it sits a theme system: colour calculation that derives
a coherent palette from a small input, per-section assignment, fonts, corner
radii.

<Artifact caption="Section composition: ready layouts, the swappable primary element, and the configurable main block that slots into each one." ratio="16/9" />

The result is worth stating flatly, because it is the whole design goal. People
built beautiful pages easily, and could not build broken ones.

<Artifact caption="The theme system: colour calculation from a small input set, per-section assignment, and the type and radius controls." ratio="4/3" />

<Aside>

The cost of this approach is real and should not be skipped over. A customer who
genuinely needs something outside the boundaries has no path at all, and the
design accepts that on purpose. What makes it defensible is that the boundaries
were drawn from what customers were already building, and that the team kept
moving them — see the method below. A constrained builder that nobody maintains
becomes a straitjacket.

</Aside>

This is the same principle I applied years later to review steps and to survey
configuration. Primitives with boundaries, five years earlier, with commercial
proof behind it.

</Beat>

<Beat n={4} title="Where the domain knowledge went">

Three things mattered more than the rest.

**Native page translation, first on the market.** DACH and Benelux meant most
customers ran two or three languages and needed career sites in all of them. The
common way to handle that is to treat a translation as a duplicate site, which
forces every language to look the same and makes each one a separate maintenance
burden. Here translation lived on the element: every element could carry a
German version, and layouts and images could differ per language. Links
translated separately, which had a useful consequence — a customer who did not
want a translated page could build a genuinely different page in another
language and translate only the links between them.

<Artifact caption="A page in two languages: per-element translation, per-language layout and imagery, and link translation handled separately from content." ratio="16/9" />

**Job prefiltering.** This one was my idea. A job section filters which jobs it
shows, which turns one section type into many pages: German jobs here, Dutch
there, each surrounded by team galleries, perk lists, a video from a team
manager. The filter is a property of the placement rather than of the section,
so composition comes for free. The evidence that the abstraction was right is
that it extended later to internal pages and internal recruitment without being
modified. A different audience and a different purpose, and the mechanism did
not need to know.

**Native cookie handling with no third parties.** Minimal cookies, cookieless
analytics. On a careers site the visitor is a candidate, and asking a candidate
to accept a wall of trackers before they can read about the job is a poor first
impression made on the customer's behalf, in a context where the customer is
trying to be liked.

</Beat>

<Beat n={5} title="What it did">

The job-alert newsletter was built natively because customers were leaving to
get it elsewhere. A visitor signs up and receives new jobs on a regular
interval, free. 60% of companies adopted it.

That was a defensive build. The feature existed elsewhere and customers were
willing to move for it, which makes the adoption figure a measure of demand the
product had been losing rather than demand it created.

Over five years, roughly every third customer cited the builder as a reason for
buying the ATS.

The second number is the one that matters, and it is the harder thing to
achieve. Adoption says an existing customer found a feature useful. A purchase
reason says the builder was doing sales work: it was part of why the deal
closed, for a third of the book, sustained across five years rather than
concentrated in one good patch.

Customers also stayed, or declined to switch, because their site was built here.
That is lock-in produced by value rather than by contract, and it is the only
kind worth engineering for, because it survives the renewal conversation without
anyone having to win it.

### How customization requests were handled

The team neither refused nor conceded. A request for custom CSS was treated as a
measurement signal: a lot of customers are writing CSS, so go and read what they
write. It was colour overrides and section reversal. Three concrete missing
things, rather than a general appetite for freedom. The answer was subthemes and
variants.

The failure mode this avoids is common enough to be worth spelling out. A team
sees the volume of workarounds, concludes that customers want flexibility, and
builds a canvas, which is how you arrive back at the product this case opens
with. The volume is the wrong signal. The content of the workaround is the
signal, and it is usually a much shorter list than the volume suggests.

<Pullquote>

When customers work around your product, don't look at the fact that they do.
Look at exactly what they patch. That's your backlog, written in their hand.

</Pullquote>

</Beat>

<Beat n={6} title="The pause">

The product did not stall and the strategy did not change. Another team needed
their project overturned and took me onto it, and the builder was frozen to
maintenance.

What was ready and unreleased at that moment was substantial. A full block
builder, with every section rewritten as a real block and every layout a genuine
block layout, locked, with user-generated layouts planned next. Advanced themes
and subthemes: each section able to use a different theme or an auto-generated
variant, to override single colours from the library, or to use prepared
subthemes for reversed and primary sections. The roadmap behind that held
referrals, an intranet, and multiple sites.

So the product was frozen at the point where it was in its strongest state and
had the least pressure on it to be released.

</Beat>

<Beat n={7} title="What I'd do differently" final={true}>

None of it shipped. It was ready. What was missing was a reason to ship it:
there was no rush, and the team kept discussing the ideal version, because
customers were delighted with what they already had.

A product that works well generates no deadline, and so finished work can sit
indefinitely. Customer satisfaction financed the delay.

The asymmetry is what I would watch for now. A struggling product ships because
it has to. A product people love ships when someone decides to, and if nobody is
made responsible for deciding, the ideal version is always one conversation
away. I would have set a release date early and defended it against my own
appetite for a better version, which is the appetite that kept the work in the
drawer.

</Beat>
