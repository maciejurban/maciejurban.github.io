The proposal was to collapse proficiency into a simpler rating. On its face that
is a simplification, and simplifications in an evaluation product usually
deserve a hearing, because every additional level is a level that managers have
to apply consistently across a whole team and across each other.

This one changed what was being measured.

Two questions live underneath a proficiency rating, and they are not the same
question:

1. **Does this person meet the current expectation for this position?**
2. **What is this person's actual proficiency in this skill?**

The first is bounded by the position model, and it cannot see below the
requirement, because everything below the requirement resolves to the same
answer. A person one step short and a person four steps short become
indistinguishable. That removes exactly the two things an organization most
wants from skill data: where the gaps are, and whether they are closing.

There is a second structural difference, and it shows up over time. Proficiency
is a property of the person. Meeting the expectation is a relation between the
person and a position, so it moves when the position moves. Promote someone and
the position-relative reading drops, though nothing about their skill has
changed; rewrite a position's requirements and every historical rating attached
to it silently means something new. One of these two measurements is stable
enough to build a trend line on. The other is not, and it will not announce
which of the two things changed.

The next effect is slower and harder to reverse. Asking the position question
repeatedly trains assessors to normalize against the position model rather than
against the skill itself. "Good enough" quietly becomes the unit of measurement.
After a year of that, the organization is holding a compliance dataset with a
skill dataset's name on it, and there is no way to tell the two apart from the
inside.

<Artifact caption="The two questions resolving to one model: the position-relative question used in a lightweight round, and the proficiency selection used in a formal review." ratio="16/9" />

The fix does not require choosing between them. Put the semantics at the
question level. A lightweight 360 asks whether someone is at the expected level
for their position, which is cheap to answer and appropriate for frequent,
low-stakes rounds. A formal review selects the specific proficiency, with full
context in front of the assessor. Same architecture underneath, different
questions on top, and every answer carries the meaning of the question that
produced it.

The pressure behind the original request was real. A full ladder is expensive to
answer honestly, and people asked to do expensive things at volume will find a
way to do them cheaply. But that cost is an incentive problem, and the ladder is
the measurement. Do not solve an incentive problem by corrupting the
measurement. If the organization wants to ask a lower-stakes question, name that
question explicitly and record which question was asked.

<Aside>

Simpler constructs are sometimes legitimately right. A short scale is a
defensible instrument, and demanding precision that assessors cannot supply
produces confident numbers with nothing behind them. The failure mode here is
narrower than "simplification is bad". It is hiding information while presenting
the result as the same measurement. A shorter scale that everyone understands to
be a shorter scale is fine. A shorter scale labelled as proficiency is not.

</Aside>

The concept was accepted for implementation and is queued. The outcome is not
really a feature. It is that the data still means what it means, and whoever
builds this later will not have to unwind a shortened semantics that has already
collected a year of answers underneath it.
